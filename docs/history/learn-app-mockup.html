TA · Knowledge in weights · 1:13

Your phone keyboard has no list of your sentences in it. It has a pile of numbers that were nudged, over millions of messages, until its guesses got good. Type “I’ll be there in five” and it offers “minutes” — not because “minutes” is filed anywhere, but because the numbers came out that way. An LLM is the same trick at scale. Nothing is stored and looked up. Training left the weights in a shape that makes true-ish continuations more likely than false ones. That is why a wrong answer arrives in the same confident voice as a right one: there is no lookup that could miss.

Check this · 1:20

Low temperature makes it repeatable, not right. At 0 it always takes its top candidate — so if the top one is wrong, you get that same wrong answer every single time, and with more confidence. Accuracy comes from the weights and the context; temperature only decides how far down the list it is willing to reach.

Where the answers come from

The brain

When the TA answers a doubt, it is not answering from the model’s own memory. Before you ever watch, I write about forty short notes — one per idea in Milestones 1 and 2 — each pinned to the moment in the lecture where Karpathy actually says it. When a doubt lands, the TA finds the two or three notes that fit and writes its reply from those notes, in your words. That is why a small OSS model is enough: it is rephrasing something already correct, not working it out.

This is one of those forty notes, for tokenization, exactly as I would write it. Every answer the TA gives is built out of notes like this — so if this one reads wrong, they all will. That is what I need you to judge.

NoteTokenization · Milestone 1

Text is chopped into a fixed vocabulary of pieces, and every piece becomes an integer id. That is the only thing the model ever sees.

Your keyboard does not think in letters and it does not think in words. It has a fixed dictionary of pieces — some are whole common words, some are fragments, some are a space plus a word — and it chops whatever you type into those pieces. Nothing outside the dictionary can be represented, so an unusual word gets broken into several pieces rather than rejected. Each piece has an id, and the id is arbitrary: it carries no meaning, it is a seat number. This is why a model’s cost is counted in tokens rather than words, why identifiers like SetMethods cost more than they look, and why a model can be strangely bad at spelling — it never saw the letters as letters.

The wrong version it watches for

“A token is a word.” Common enough that it survives a long time. It breaks the moment you paste code or a rare name and the count triples.

If you overrule a flag, that is a strong signal — either the brain is wrong or the misconception is deep. It could ride along in the end-of-session digest, or stay entirely local.
